If you can only fully prepare four, prepare **one from each of these**:
- **#1 or #5** (technology — your natural strength; write it and you dominate)

### Model Essay 1 — *Artificial Intelligence: Opportunity or Threat?*
**(Theme: technology · ~940 words · Skeleton B, 7 paragraphs)**

In 1950 Alan Turing asked whether a machine could think, and treated the question as
one for the distant future. Seventy-five years later the question has quietly changed
its form. Machines are already writing text, diagnosing disease, driving vehicles and
deciding whose loan application is approved. The debate is no longer whether artificial
intelligence will arrive but on what terms it will be allowed to reshape work, learning
and government. This essay argues that artificial intelligence is neither an
opportunity nor a threat in itself; it is an amplifier, and what it amplifies depends
almost entirely on the institutions that govern it. It examines the technology's
promise, its risks to employment and to fairness, its implications for a country like
India and for a region like the North-East, and finally the shape a sensible regulatory
response might take.

Artificial intelligence is not a single invention but a family of techniques by which
computers improve their performance at a task by learning from data rather than by
following instructions written out in advance. The idea is old; what is new is the
combination of very large datasets, cheap computing power and improved learning
methods. Where earlier waves of automation replaced muscle, this one substitutes for
certain kinds of judgement. That is why its social consequences are harder to predict
than those of the steam engine or the assembly line.

The opportunity is real and, in a developing country, unusually large. India suffers
from a chronic shortage of specialists rather than of people. There are too few doctors
for its villages, too few trained teachers for its classrooms, and too few technical
staff for its courts and offices. Artificial intelligence does not remove the need for
these professionals, but it can extend their reach. A trained system that flags
suspicious images can allow one radiologist to screen many more patients. A translation
system can deliver a government scheme's terms in a language the applicant actually
speaks — a matter of real consequence in a country with twenty-two scheduled languages
and hundreds more in daily use. In agriculture, forecasts based on local weather and
soil data can help a farmer decide when to sow. These are not futuristic scenarios;
they are extensions of what already works.

Against this stands the question of employment, which is the anxiety most often voiced
and the one least well understood. Automation has historically destroyed particular
jobs while creating others, and the net effect over a century has been more employment,
not less. But the transition is neither automatic nor painless, and it is not
distributed evenly. The Indian services sector, which absorbed a generation of
educated young people into routine data and support work, is precisely the kind of
employment that current systems perform competently. A young graduate whose skill was
the accurate processing of routine information now competes with a machine that does it
faster. The remedy is not to obstruct the technology, which is impossible, but to
change what education produces — and India's record on retraining adults in mid-career
is weak.

A second and subtler risk is the question of fairness. A learning system reproduces the
patterns in the data it is given. If historical hiring records reflect prejudice, a
system trained on them will reproduce that prejudice while wearing the appearance of
objectivity — which makes it harder, not easier, to challenge. When such systems are
used in policing, in credit, or in the allocation of welfare, the consequences fall
hardest on those least able to appeal. There is also the matter of privacy: these
systems are hungry for personal data, and India's framework for protecting it is still
maturing. A decision made by a machine that cannot explain itself sits uneasily with
the principle, fundamental to administrative law, that a citizen is entitled to reasons.

The regional dimension deserves particular attention. In Nagaland and the wider
North-East, the promise of artificial intelligence is bound up with a prior problem:
connectivity. A technology delivered over the internet is useless where the internet is
intermittent, and hill terrain makes networks expensive to build and easy to lose.
There is therefore a real danger that a technology capable of narrowing the gap between
a metropolitan hospital and a district one will instead widen it, simply because the
infrastructure arrives late. At the same time the region has assets that matter here:
a young, English-educated population well placed to work in software services, and a
linguistic diversity that makes it an important place for language technology if that
work is done with, rather than merely about, local communities. Whether artificial
intelligence helps or hurts the North-East will be settled less by the technology than
by investment in fibre, electricity and skills.

What follows for policy? Three things, none of them exotic. First, regulation should
attach to the *use* rather than to the technology: a system that decides on a loan or a
criminal charge should be held to standards of explanation and appeal, whoever built
it. Second, education must shift from the transmission of retrievable information —
which machines now supply on demand — towards judgement, communication and the ethical
handling of problems, which they do not. Third, public investment should go where the
market will not: rural connectivity, Indian-language datasets, and the retraining of
workers whose jobs change under them.

Artificial intelligence, then, is best understood as a mirror with a magnifying glass
attached. It reflects the priorities of the society that builds it and enlarges the
consequences. A country that deploys it to widen access to medicine and schooling will
find it an extraordinary opportunity; one that deploys it without rules, in an
environment of unequal infrastructure and weak accountability, will find it deepening
divisions it already has. The technology will not choose between these outcomes. We
will.

*(≈940 words)*

| Move | Where | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Concrete historical hook** — Turing, 1950 | ¶1 line 1 | Avoids "in this modern era of…". Signals reading. |
| **Explicit thesis sentence** | ¶1: *"…is an amplifier…"* | The examiner can point to your argument. Worth 2 marks on its own. |
| **Roadmap sentence** | end of ¶1 | Tells the examiner the essay is planned. |
| **Definition paragraph** | ¶2 | Shows you know what you are talking about before you evaluate it. |
| **PESTLE in action** | ¶3 Social, ¶4 Economic, ¶5 Legal/Ethical, ¶6 Regional, ¶7 Political | Six lenses, six paragraphs. This is the framework doing the work. |
| **Balance — the "however"** | ¶4 opens *"Against this stands…"* | Two-sided treatment. Never a rant. |
| **Nagaland paragraph** | ¶6 | The differentiator. Almost nobody does this. |
| **Facts that cannot be wrong** | "twenty-two scheduled languages" | A safe, checkable constitutional fact. No invented statistics anywhere. |
| **Concrete way-forward** | ¶7, numbered "First… Second… Third…" | Specific, actionable, not "the government should take steps". |
| **Conditional optimistic close** | last ¶ | Mature. Ends on an image ("mirror with a magnifying glass") and a short punch line. |

### Model Essay 2 — *Nagaland at Sixty: Achievements and Challenges*
**(Theme: Nagaland / North-East · ~890 words · Skeleton B)**

Nagaland became the sixteenth State of the Indian Union on 1 December 1963, the first
state in the North-East to be carved out after independence and, at the time, an
experiment whose outcome no one could be confident of. Six decades later the experiment
has produced a society with near-universal literacy by regional standards, a
constitutional settlement unlike any other in the country, and a young population
better educated than its parents. It has also produced persistent unemployment, a
narrow economy and an infrastructure deficit that constrains almost everything else.
This essay argues that Nagaland's central achievement has been political and cultural —
the preservation of a distinct identity inside a federal union — and that its central
unfinished task is economic. It considers the constitutional foundation, the social
gains, the economic constraints, and what a realistic path forward might look like.

The state's foundation is unusual and worth understanding precisely. Statehood followed
the Sixteen Point Agreement between the Naga People's Convention and the Government of
India, and was accompanied by the insertion of **Article 371A** into the Constitution
by the Thirteenth Amendment in 1962. Under that article, no Act of Parliament applies
in Nagaland in respect of the religious or social practices of the Nagas, Naga
customary law and procedure, the administration of civil and criminal justice involving
decisions according to customary law, or the ownership and transfer of land and its
resources, unless the Legislative Assembly of Nagaland resolves that it shall. Few
provisions in the Indian Constitution grant a state legislature so direct a veto. Its
existence is why customary institutions — village councils, community land holding,
traditional dispute resolution — have survived not as folklore but as functioning law.

That constitutional space has produced real social achievement. Literacy in Nagaland is
high by national standards, and the participation of women in education has been
substantial. The state has an unusually dense fabric of community institutions: village
councils that command genuine authority, church-based organisations with wide reach, and
village development boards through which local decisions about small assets are
actually taken locally. The most distinctive expression of this is the state's
**communitisation** policy — the Nagaland Communitisation of Public Institutions and
Services Act, 2002 — under which the management of primary schools, health centres and
electricity distribution at village level was transferred to community committees.
The idea that a village should own the school it sends its children to is not a slogan
here but a statute. It is a genuine contribution to Indian administrative thinking and
deserves to be better known outside the region.

Culturally, the state has managed something that has eluded many places: it has made
tradition legible to outsiders without hollowing it out. The Hornbill Festival,
held annually at Kisama near Kohima, has grown from a state initiative into the
region's best-known cultural event, drawing visitors from across India and abroad. It
has given the sixteen recognised tribes a shared platform without dissolving what
distinguishes them. Whatever one thinks of the tension between performance and
authenticity, the festival has done more for the state's external image than any
publicity campaign could have.

The challenges are, by contrast, stubbornly economic. Nagaland's economy remains
narrow. Agriculture, much of it still practised as jhum or shifting cultivation on hill
slopes, supports a large share of households at low productivity. Manufacturing is
minimal. The organised sector is dominated by government employment, which is finite
and already saturated, and this single fact explains a great deal about the state's
social anxieties. Each year the colleges produce graduates whose ambitions were formed
around a government job that no longer exists in sufficient numbers, and the result is
educated unemployment — a form of unemployment that is politically and socially more
combustible than the ordinary kind. Terrain compounds everything: roads are expensive
to build and are cut by the monsoon, which raises the cost of every good that enters or
leaves the state and quietly taxes every business.

What, realistically, is the way forward? First, connectivity must be treated as the
precondition for everything else rather than as one sector among many; road, rail, air
and digital links determine whether any other policy can work. Second, the state's
comparative advantages should be taken seriously rather than romanticised. Horticulture
and organic produce, bamboo, tourism and the services sector — the last of which
depends on educated English-speaking youth and reliable bandwidth rather than on
factories or ports — are the plausible growth areas for a landlocked hill state. Third,
education must shift its centre of gravity from preparing candidates for examinations
to preparing them for enterprise, which means vocational training and access to credit,
not exhortation. Fourth, the **Act East Policy** gives the region a strategic role as
India's land bridge to South-East Asia; Nagaland's border with Myanmar is an asset only
if trade infrastructure and security are addressed together.

At sixty, Nagaland's record is therefore genuinely mixed, and honestly so. It has
protected what it set out to protect: land, custom, identity, and a form of local
democracy that most of India talks about and few practise. What it has not yet built is
an economy capable of employing the confident, literate generation those institutions
produced. The task of the next decade is not to choose between tradition and
development — that framing has always been false here — but to make the state's
distinctive institutions the instrument of its development rather than a consolation
for the lack of it.

*(≈890 words)*

#### Why this scores

| Move | Where | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **A precise, checkable opening fact** | 1 Dec 1963, sixteenth state | Immediate authority. Not a vague generalisation. |
| **Balanced thesis stated outright** | ¶1: achievement political/cultural, unfinished task economic | Gives the essay a spine and a built-in structure. |
| **Constitutional detail with the right keywords** | ¶2, Art. 371A, four protected subjects | This is exactly what a Nagaland examiner is looking for. Doubles as GK revision. |
| **A distinctive, under-used example** | Communitisation Act, 2002 | Shows genuine local knowledge. Very few candidates will have this. |
| **Cultural paragraph with a real institution** | Hornbill Festival, Kisama | Concrete, verifiable, regional. |
| **Honest treatment of problems** | ¶5, educated unemployment | Not defensive, not despairing. Examiners reward candour. |
| **Numbered, concrete way forward** | ¶6 | Four specific levers, each tied to a stated constraint. |
| **Reframing conclusion** | last ¶: rejects the "tradition vs development" binary | Refusing a false dichotomy is the single most reliable way to sound mature. |
| **No invented statistics anywhere** | throughout | "high by national standards", "a large share" — qualitative, safe, unfalsifiable. |

### Model Essay 3 — *Climate Change and India's Development Dilemma*
**(Theme: environment · ~880 words · Skeleton B)**

India occupies an uncomfortable position in the world's climate arithmetic. It is among
the countries most exposed to the physical consequences of a warming planet — a long
coastline, a monsoon-dependent agriculture, Himalayan glaciers feeding its northern
rivers — and simultaneously a country whose per capita energy use remains far below the
levels the industrialised world has long taken for granted. To ask India to constrain
its emissions is to ask a country still lifting hundreds of millions of people out of
poverty to accept a limit that no wealthy nation accepted while it was growing. This
essay argues that the apparent conflict between development and climate action is real
in the short term but false in the long one, and that India's interest lies in leading
the transition rather than resisting it. It considers the nature of the exposure, the
equity argument, the economic case for early action, the regional dimension in the
North-East, and the institutional steps required.

The physical exposure comes first because it is not a matter of opinion. Indian
agriculture remains substantially rain-fed, and the monsoon is the single variable on
which the rural economy turns. A warming atmosphere holds more moisture, which tends to
concentrate rainfall into fewer, heavier events — more flood, more drought, less of the
steady rain that crops need. Sea-level rise threatens coastal cities and the
agricultural deltas behind them. Retreating glaciers alter the flow of rivers on which
the northern plains depend. Heat itself is a direct threat: outdoor labour, which is
how a large part of India earns, becomes dangerous above certain temperatures. These
are not distant projections but processes already underway.

Against this, the equity argument retains its force and should not be abandoned out of
embarrassment. The stock of greenhouse gases now in the atmosphere was placed there
overwhelmingly by countries that industrialised first. India's per capita emissions
remain well below the global average. The principle of **common but differentiated
responsibilities**, embedded in international climate agreements, exists precisely to
recognise this asymmetry. A poor country that has contributed least to a problem cannot
reasonably be asked to bear the largest share of the cost of solving it, and India is
right to insist on finance and technology transfer as the condition of ambition.

Yet equity is an argument about *who pays*, not about *whether to act* — and here the
economic case has changed decisively. For most of the last century, cleaner energy
meant costlier energy, and a developing country choosing it accepted slower growth. That
trade-off has weakened. The cost of solar generation has fallen dramatically over the
past fifteen years, and India, with high solar irradiance across much of its territory,
is unusually well placed to benefit. There is also a balance-of-payments argument that
has nothing to do with the climate at all: India imports a large share of the crude oil
it consumes, and every unit of energy generated domestically from sun or wind is a unit
not purchased in foreign currency. Energy security and decarbonisation point in the same
direction, which is a fortunate and unusual alignment. Add to this the health cost of
air pollution in Indian cities — measured in illness and lost working days — and early
action begins to look less like a sacrifice than an investment.

The North-East illustrates both the stakes and the complications. The region carries a
large share of India's remaining forest cover, and its watersheds feed the Brahmaputra
system on which Assam's agriculture depends. Its hills are geologically young, heavily
rained upon and prone to landslides, so intensified rainfall translates quickly into
destroyed roads and isolated villages. At the same time, the region's people are asked
to conserve forests whose benefits — carbon storage, water regulation, biodiversity —
are enjoyed nationally while the cost of foregone livelihood is borne locally. Any
serious climate policy must therefore include payment for these services, in the form of
compensatory transfers and support for alternatives to shifting cultivation that are
attractive rather than merely mandated. Conservation that ignores the household economy
of the conserving community does not last.

What should be done? Four measures stand out. First, accelerate the shift to renewable
generation while investing seriously in storage and grid capacity, without which
renewable capacity is stranded. Second, treat adaptation as equal in status to
mitigation — drought-resistant crops, restored wetlands, climate-resilient road and
drainage design in the hills, and early-warning systems. Third, price carbon-intensive
choices honestly while protecting the poor from the consequences, since a transition
perceived as unfair will not survive politically. Fourth, continue to press
internationally for climate finance, because a country that has not caused a problem
should not have to solve it alone.

The dilemma, then, is genuine but narrower than it appears. India cannot and should not
stop growing, and no responsible argument asks it to. What it can do is choose the
technology of its growth, and here the interests of development and of the climate have
converged more than they have diverged. A country that builds its next fifty years on
imported fossil fuel will be poorer, sicker and more exposed than one that builds them
on domestic sunlight. The question is not whether India can afford to act on climate
change. It is whether it can afford the alternative.

*(≈880 words)*

| Move | Where | Effect |
|---|---|---|

| **Thesis takes a position** | ¶1: conflict real short-term, false long-term | Avoids fence-sitting while remaining balanced. |

### Model Essay 4 — *Unemployment among the Educated Youth of Nagaland*
**(Theme: economy + Nagaland · ~860 words · Skeleton B condensed to 6 paragraphs)**

There is a particular kind of frustration that belongs to the educated unemployed. It is
not the frustration of the person who never had a chance, but of the person who did
everything that was asked — finished school, finished college, sat the examinations —
and found at the end of it that the door which was supposed to open is not there. In
Nagaland this experience has become common enough to shape a generation's outlook. This
essay argues that educated unemployment in the state is not a failure of individual
effort or even, principally, of education, but a structural mismatch between an economy
with only one large employer and an education system built to supply that employer. It
examines how the situation arose, why the usual remedies have underperformed, and what
a realistic alternative would involve.

The origin of the problem lies in the shape of the state's economy. Nagaland is
landlocked and mountainous, with a small internal market and high transport costs.
Manufacturing has never established itself at scale, and agriculture, much of it still
practised as shifting cultivation on hill slopes, absorbs many households at low
productivity and provides little surplus for reinvestment. The consequence is that the
organised, salaried sector is dominated by government — departments, schools, the
police, public undertakings. For a long period this worked well enough: government
employment grew, and a college degree was a reasonable route into it. But a government
payroll cannot grow indefinitely, and it has not. The number of graduates has continued
to rise while the number of posts has not, and the gap between the two is precisely the
problem.

Education has adapted to this reality in exactly the wrong way. When the only prize
worth having is a government job, schooling and college orient themselves towards the
qualifications and examinations that lead to it. General-degree enrolment is high;
vocational and technical enrolment is comparatively low. Students acquire credentials
rather than capabilities, and employers outside government — where they exist —
complain of graduates who are literate, willing and untrained. There is also a social
dimension. A government post carries prestige and security that private employment,
especially manual or entrepreneurial work, does not, and families invest in the pursuit
of that prestige. The result is a queue: young people spending years preparing for
competitive examinations rather than entering a labour market they perceive as beneath
their qualification. Every year spent in the queue is a year of skill not acquired.

The usual remedies have not been useless, but they have underperformed for identifiable
reasons. Skill-development programmes have often trained people for trades for which
there was no local demand, so that the trained migrate or return to waiting.
Self-employment and credit schemes founder where collateral is difficult, since land in
Nagaland is largely held under community and customary tenure and is not readily
mortgageable — a genuine constraint that arises directly from the land provisions
protected under Article 371A, and one that deserves a designed solution rather than
lament. And enterprise of any kind runs into the same physical facts that hold back
everything else: unreliable power, roads damaged each monsoon, and freight costs that
make it hard to sell outward.

What would a serious alternative look like? Four elements. **First, connectivity and
power, treated as employment policy rather than as infrastructure policy.** No
enterprise strategy survives an eight-hour outage or a road closed for three weeks;
these are not background conditions but the binding constraint. **Second, sectors chosen
for fit rather than fashion.** A landlocked hill state with high rainfall, forest cover
and a young English-speaking population has real advantages in horticulture and organic
produce, bamboo and allied processing, tourism built around the state's cultural
distinctiveness, and remote-delivered services — the last requiring bandwidth and
training rather than factories or ports, and therefore the most immediately achievable.
**Third, credit that works with customary tenure rather than against it** — group
guarantees, self-help-group lending, cooperative and community-backed instruments — so
that the absence of mortgageable land stops being an absolute bar. **Fourth, a shift in
what education produces**, with apprenticeship and vocational streams given genuine
parity of esteem, and entrepreneurship taught as a practical subject rather than
celebrated in speeches.

None of this is quick, and it would be dishonest to suggest otherwise. But the
alternative — a growing cohort of qualified young people queueing for a shrinking number
of posts — is not stable. Educated unemployment does not merely waste the public money
already spent on schooling; it corrodes confidence in institutions and, where other
outlets are absent, feeds substance abuse and disaffection. Nagaland has assets that
many states lack: high literacy, dense and functioning community institutions, a
distinctive culture with real economic potential, and constitutional protection for its
land and custom. The task is to convert those assets from things to be preserved into
things that generate livelihoods. That conversion is a matter of policy design and
sustained investment, not of exhorting young people to lower their expectations. They
did what was asked of them. The economy now has to hold up its side of the bargain.

*(≈860 words)*

| Move | Where | Effect |
|---|---|---|

## 4. Opening patterns

Adapt these. Each is a *shape*, not a script.

| # | Pattern | Template | Example |
|:---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Historical anchor** | "In [year], [person] [did/asked something]. [Contrast with today.]" | "In 1950 Alan Turing asked whether a machine could think, and treated it as a question for the distant future." |
| 2 | **Stark contrast** | "India [impressive fact], and yet [troubling fact]." | "India sends missions to the Moon while a third of its children remain undernourished." |
| 3 | **Definition** | "[Term] means [plain definition]. But the word has come to carry more than that." | "Good governance means the exercise of public authority in a way that is lawful, accountable and responsive." |
| 4 | **The paradox** | "[X] is at once [A] and [B]." | "Nagaland is at once one of India's most literate states and one of its least industrialised." |
| 5 | **The scene** | "[Concrete image, one sentence.] [Zoom out to the general problem.]" | "Every monsoon, roads in the hill districts of the North-East that took three years to build vanish in a single night." |
| 6 | **The question** | "[Direct question]. The answer determines [large consequence]." | "Who should own the data that a citizen generates simply by living?" |
| 7 | **The quotation** | "'[Quote]', said [person]. [Apply to topic in one sentence.]" | "'Knowledge is power,' wrote Francis Bacon — a claim that reads differently in an age of unlimited information." |
| 8 | **The uncomfortable position** | "[Country/state] occupies an uncomfortable position in [X]." | "India occupies an uncomfortable position in the world's climate arithmetic." |
| 9 | **The human** | "There is a particular kind of [emotion] that belongs to [group]." | "There is a particular kind of frustration that belongs to the educated unemployed." |
| 10 | **The date** | "[Place] became [X] on [date]. [Number] years later, [assessment]." | "Nagaland became the sixteenth State of the Indian Union on 1 December 1963." |

**Thesis-sentence templates** (put one at the end of ¶1 — always):

- "This essay argues that X is not primarily a problem of A but of B."
- "The central contention of this essay is that the conflict between A and B is real in the short term but false in the long one."
- "It will be argued that X is neither a pure opportunity nor a pure threat, but an amplifier of the institutions that govern it."
- "This essay contends that the achievement has been A, and that the unfinished task is B."

**Roadmap-sentence templates:**

- "It examines the economic, social and governance dimensions in turn, before turning to the specific case of the North-East."
- "It considers first how the situation arose, then why the usual remedies have underperformed, and finally what an alternative would involve."
- "The discussion proceeds in four parts: context, causes, consequences and the way forward."

## 5. Closing patterns

| # | Pattern | Template |
|:---:|---|---|
| 1 | **Synthesis + condition** | "The record is therefore mixed: [gain] alongside [gap]. Whether [X] proves [good] or [bad] depends less on [surface factor] than on [real factor]." |
| 2 | **Refuse the false binary** | "The task is not to choose between A and B — that framing has always been false — but to make A the instrument of B." |
| 3 | **Rhetorical inversion** | "The question is not whether we can afford to [act]. It is whether we can afford the alternative." |
| 4 | **The agent named** | "[The technology / the policy / the law] will not choose between these outcomes. We will." |
| 5 | **The obligation** | "They did what was asked of them. [The institution] now has to hold up its side of the bargain." |
| 6 | **Image callback** | Return in one line to the image you opened with, now transformed by the argument. |
| 7 | **Conditional optimism** | "There is reason for confidence, provided that [specific condition] is met. Without it, [X] will remain a statement of intent rather than a description of reality." |
| 8 | **Quotation close** | "[Person]'s warning that '[quote]' has lost none of its force." *(only if it genuinely fits)* |

**Never close with:** "Thus we can conclude that…", "Last but not least…", "In a
nutshell…", "Hence India will surely become a superpower by 20XX."

## 6. Connectives

Vary these. Repeating "moreover" five times is a visible weakness.

| Function | Options |
|---|---|
| **Adding** | Moreover · Furthermore · In addition · Equally important · A second consideration is · What is more |
| **Contrasting** | However · Yet · Against this stands · By contrast · Nevertheless · On the other hand · That said · Even so |
| **Conceding then turning** | While it is true that X, … · Granting that X, it remains the case that Y · X is real, but it is narrower than it appears |
| **Causing** | Consequently · As a result · It follows that · This explains why · Hence |
| **Illustrating** | For instance · A clear example is · Consider the case of · This is visible in |
| **Sequencing** | First · Second · Third · Finally · The first of these · Turning now to |
| **Emphasising** | Crucially · What matters most is · The decisive point is · Above all |
| **Qualifying** | In part · To an extent · Broadly · With some exceptions · Under certain conditions |
| **Concluding a paragraph** | The upshot is that · In short · What this means in practice is |

## 7. How to use this file

| Days | Action |
|---|---|
| **1–3** | Read all four model essays. For each, cover the annotation table and try to identify the moves yourself first, then check. Copy out **one introduction and one conclusion by hand** — the rhythm has to enter your hand, not just your eye. |
| **4–14** | **Five timed essays**, one every alternate day, 50 minutes each, handwritten, from the topic list. Rotate themes: technology → Nagaland → environment → social → economy. On the off days, do a **10-minute plan-only drill**: pick a topic, do the 8-minute PESTLE plan, stop. Eight to ten of these. |
| **15–19** | **Two more timed essays**, at least one Nagaland/North-East. Then re-read your five earlier essays and note your three recurring weaknesses (usually: paragraph too long, no way-forward, weak conclusion). Fix those three specifically. |
| **20** | Read the quotation bank and the opening/closing patterns once. Write out on one card: your two favourite openings, your two favourite closings, three quotations, the PESTLE letters. |
| **21** | Read that one card. Nothing else. |

### Self-marking rubric for your practice essays

Score yourself out of 20 honestly:

- [ ] **Relevance (4)** — did I address *every* clause of the exact topic?
- [ ] **Thesis (2)** — is there one sentence in ¶1 stating my argument?
- [ ] **Structure (4)** — 6–9 paragraphs, one idea each, in a logical order?
- [ ] **Evidence (3)** — at least three concrete anchors (article, scheme, institution, example)? Zero invented numbers?
- [ ] **Balance (2)** — is there a genuine "however" paragraph?
- [ ] **Way forward (2)** — specific and actionable, not "the government should take steps"?
- [ ] **Language (2)** — varied sentence length, no repeated connectives, formal register?
- [ ] **Presentation (1)** — margin, paragraph breaks, title underlined, legible?

Below 12 means fix structure. Above 15 in practice usually means 13–14 in the hall —
which is a very good essay mark.
