# Paper 1 — General English & General Knowledge: The Playbook

> **Exam:** NPSC Combined Technical Services Examination (CTSE) 2026
> **Paper:** General English & General Knowledge (Combined) — **100 marks, 3 hours**
> **Date:** 26 September 2026, 09:00–12:00
> **Written for:** a B.Tech CS candidate with a strong technical base and limited humanities practice.

This is the **one-shot master guide**. If you only read one file, read this one.
The other two files (`02_ESSAY_BANK.md`, `03_GK_CAPSULE.md`) are the raw material this
playbook tells you how to use.

---

## 0. What the paper actually is (read this first)

Straight from the official pattern (`Materials/_Shared/OFFICIAL_SYLLABUS.md`, §1):

| # | Component | Marks | It tests |
|---|-----------|------:|----------|
| 2.1.1 | **Essay** (1 essay) | **20** | Sustained written argument in English |
| 2.1.2 | **Précis writing** (1 passage) | **10** | Comprehension + compression |
| 2.1.3 | **Amplification** (1 maxim/proverb) | **10** | Interpretation + illustration |
| 2.1.4 | **Construction of sentence & Grammar** | **10** | Mechanical accuracy |
| 2.2 | **General Knowledge** | **50** | Current events, general science, mental ability, Indian history, Indian polity, Indian economy, Geography — *"combination of objective type and short descriptive answers"* |
| | **Total** | **100** | in **180 minutes** |

### Three consequences you must internalise

1. **Half the paper is GK, and GK is the only half where marks are binary.**
   In English, a mediocre essay still gets ~9–11/20. In GK, you either know the answer
   or you do not. So GK is where your 21 days of *study* go; English is where your
   21 days of *practice* go. They are different activities. Do both.

2. **English is scored generously in the middle and stingily at the top.**
   Almost nobody scores 17/20 on the essay. Almost everybody who writes 900 clean,
   relevant, well-structured words scores 12–14. Your target is **not** a brilliant
   essay. Your target is a *competent, complete, legible* essay — and then to bank
   every mechanical mark in précis, amplification and grammar. That is a very
   achievable 35–40 out of 50 in English.

3. **"Combination of objective type and short descriptive answers"** means GK is
   probably split — a block of one-mark/two-mark objective items plus several
   3–5 mark short-answer questions. Prepare for both. Objective = recall of facts.
   Short descriptive = a 60–120 word structured answer. Section 6 of this file
   teaches the short-descriptive format.

## 1. Exam-day time allocation — the 180-minute plan

### 1.1 The recommended order of attempt

**Do not attempt in the order printed on the paper.** Attempt in this order:

| Order | Component | Marks | Minutes | Cumulative |
|:---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1st | **Grammar / sentence construction** | 10 | **15** | 15 |
| 2nd | **GK — objective portion** | ~20–25 | **25** | 40 |
| 3rd | **GK — short descriptive portion** | ~25–30 | **35** | 75 |
| 4th | **Amplification** | 10 | **20** | 95 |
| 5th | **Précis** | 10 | **25** | 120 |
| 6th | **Essay** | 20 | **50** | 170 |
| — | **Buffer / review / page numbering** | — | **10** | 180 |

### 1.2 Why this order

- **Grammar first (15 min).** These are the cheapest marks on the paper — pure recall,
  no thinking, no fatigue cost. Banking 8–9/10 in the first fifteen minutes settles
  your nerves and guarantees a floor. Never leave grammar until the end; if you run
  out of time you will lose marks you already knew.
- **GK second, while your memory is freshest.** Recall degrades under fatigue far
  faster than writing fluency does. Do the objective block fast — if you do not know
  an item in 20 seconds, mark it, move on, come back in the buffer.
- **Amplification and précis in the middle.** Both are *bounded* tasks — there is a
  correct-ish answer and a fixed length. They do not expand to fill available time
  the way an essay does. Doing them before the essay stops the essay from eating them.
- **Essay last, with a hard 50-minute block.** The essay is the one task where you can
  keep writing forever. Putting it last means the clock, not your judgement, ends it —
  and a 50-minute essay written at the end still scores 12–14 if you follow the
  skeleton. A rushed 15-minute précis at the end scores 4.

**One exception:** if the essay topic list is on page 1 and you can see it, *read the
essay topics in the first 60 seconds* and pick your topic. Then let it marinate in the
back of your mind while you do everything else. You will start the essay with your
argument already half-formed. This single trick is worth 2–3 marks.

### 1.3 Micro-timings inside each block

**Grammar (15 min)** — 45–60 seconds per item. Do not agonise. If two options both
"sound right", pick the shorter/simpler one and move on.

**GK objective (25 min)** — Two passes. Pass 1 (18 min): answer everything you know
instantly. Pass 2 (7 min): return to the marked ones and make an educated guess.
*There is no stated negative marking on this paper* (negative marking is specified only
for the Technical MCQ paper) — so **attempt every question**. Leave nothing blank.

**GK short descriptive (35 min)** — Budget by marks: roughly 5 minutes per 5-mark
question, 3 minutes per 3-mark. Write to length, then stop.

**Amplification (20 min)** — 3 min planning, 15 min writing, 2 min checking.

**Précis (25 min)** — 6 min reading and marking the passage, 4 min drafting in the
margin, 12 min writing the fair copy, 3 min counting words and writing the title.

**Essay (50 min)** — **8 min planning on rough paper**, 38 min writing, 4 min reading
back. The 8 minutes of planning is non-negotiable. An unplanned essay loses more than
8 minutes' worth of marks in repetition and drift.

### 1.4 The three clock checkpoints

Write these on your rough sheet the moment you get it:

```
10:00  →  Grammar done, GK objective underway
11:15  →  ALL GK done. Nothing else touched.
11:40  →  Amplification + précis done. Essay starts NOW.
12:00  →  Pens down.
```

If at 11:40 you are not on the essay, **abandon whatever you are doing and start the
essay.** Twenty marks unattempted is catastrophic; a half-finished précis is not.

## 2. ESSAY — 20 marks

The single biggest block on the paper. Fifty minutes, one-fifth of your total.

### 2.1 How state PSC essays are actually marked

Examiners rarely have a public rubric, but the working marking scheme across state
commissions is consistently something like this. Treat it as the shape of the thing,
not gospel:

| Dimension | Weight | What the examiner is looking for |
|---|---:|---|
| **Content & relevance** | ~7/20 | Do you address *this* topic? Is there real substance — facts, examples, dimensions — or padding? |
| **Structure & coherence** | ~5/20 | Intro → body with distinct paragraphs → conclusion. One idea per paragraph. Logical flow. |
| **Language & expression** | ~4/20 | Grammar, vocabulary, sentence variety, no repetition. |
| **Balance & maturity** | ~2/20 | Do you see more than one side? Is the tone measured rather than ranting? |
| **Presentation** | ~2/20 | Legibility, margins, paragraph breaks, no overwriting, a visible plan. |

**The two commonest reasons a competent candidate scores 9 instead of 14:**
- They wrote about the *general area* of the topic instead of the *exact* topic.
  ("Role of technology in governance" answered as an essay on "what is technology".)
- They wrote one enormous 800-word block with no paragraph breaks.

Both are free to fix.

### 2.2 Length

| | |
|---|---|
| **Target word count** | **800–1000 words** |
| **Absolute floor** | 600 words (below this it reads as unfinished) |
| **Ceiling** | 1200 words (beyond this you are stealing time from other sections) |
| **Rough page count** | 3.5–4.5 sides of standard answer-book paper in normal handwriting |
| **Paragraphs** | 6–9 |

Calibrate this **before the exam**: write one timed 900-word essay at home, count the
lines, and memorise "900 words = N lines in my handwriting". On exam day you then never
count words — you count lines. Saves five minutes.

### 2.3 The reusable skeletons

You will walk into the hall with **two skeletons memorised**. Whatever the topic, one
of them fits.

#### Skeleton A — the 5-paragraph (use when the topic is narrow or you are short on ideas)

| ¶ | Function | Words |
|:---:|---|---:|
| 1 | **Introduction** — hook, definition/context, your thesis, roadmap | 120 |
| 2 | **The case / the positives / the current situation** | 180 |
| 3 | **The counter-case / problems / challenges** | 180 |
| 4 | **The way forward** — concrete, specific suggestions | 180 |
| 5 | **Conclusion** — synthesis + forward-looking close | 110 |
| | **Total** | **~770** |

#### Skeleton B — the 7-paragraph (use when the topic is broad; this is your default)

| ¶ | Function | Words |
|:---:|---|---:|
| 1 | **Introduction** — hook / definition / thesis / roadmap | 130 |
| 2 | **Background & context** — how did we get here? (the *past*) | 130 |
| 3 | **Dimension 1** — usually Economic | 140 |
| 4 | **Dimension 2** — usually Social / Human | 140 |
| 5 | **Dimension 3** — usually Political/Governance, Technological, or Environmental | 140 |
| 6 | **Challenges & the balanced view** — "however…" | 140 |
| 7 | **Way forward + Conclusion** (can be split into two short paragraphs) | 150 |
| | **Total** | **~970** |

**Add a Nagaland / North-East paragraph wherever it plausibly fits.** On almost any
NPSC essay topic — digital governance, education, unemployment, environment, women,
tourism, infrastructure — you can insert one paragraph localising the argument to
Nagaland or the North-East. Examiners in a state commission notice this and reward it.
It is probably the highest-return single habit in this whole document.

### 2.4 Writing an introduction that scores

A scoring introduction does **four** things in about 120 words, in this order:

1. **Hook** — one arresting sentence. Options:
   - A short relevant quotation.
   - A stark contrast: *"India launches missions to the Moon while a third of its
     children remain stunted."*
   - A definition: *"Digital governance is the use of information technology to
     deliver public services more transparently and more cheaply."*
   - A scene: *"In a village in Nagaland's Kiphire district, a mobile signal arrives
     for two hours a day."* (Use only if plausible — do not invent a specific claim.)
2. **Context** — two sentences placing the topic in time and place.
3. **Thesis** — one sentence stating what *you* will argue. This is the sentence the
   examiner looks for. *"This essay argues that X is not primarily a technological
   problem but an institutional one."*
4. **Roadmap** — one sentence: *"It examines the economic, social and governance
   dimensions in turn, before turning to the specific case of the North-East."*

**Never** open with "Since the dawn of civilisation, man has…" or "In this modern era
of globalisation…". These are the two most common openings on any answer script and
examiners are numb to them.

### 2.5 Writing a conclusion that scores

Rules:
- **Never introduce a new argument.**
- **Never** just repeat the introduction in different words.
- Do three things in ~110 words:
  1. **Synthesise** — one sentence pulling the body together ("The record is therefore
     mixed: real gains in access, persistent gaps in quality.").
  2. **Position** — restate your thesis, now as something you have *earned*.
  3. **Forward look** — end optimistic but conditional. "…provided that…", "…if X is
     matched by Y…". A conditional optimistic close reads as mature; an unqualified
     "thus India will surely become a superpower" reads as naive.
- A short closing quotation is fine if it genuinely fits. One quotation per essay is
  plenty; three is showing off.

### 2.6 Generating content fast under pressure

This is the skill that separates a 9 from a 14 when you don't know much about the
topic. Spend your 8 planning minutes running **two frameworks** over the topic and
harvesting whatever falls out.

#### Framework 1 — PESTLE (gives you *dimensions* → your body paragraphs)

| Letter | Lens | Ask yourself |
|:---:|---|---|
| **P** | Political / Governance | Who decides? Which ministry, law, scheme? Centre vs State? |
| **E** | Economic | Cost, jobs, growth, inequality, who pays, who profits |
| **S** | Social | Families, women, youth, tribes, caste, migration, health, education |
| **T** | Technological | What technology enables or threatens this? Digital divide? |
| **L** | Legal / Ethical | Which rights are engaged? Article 21? Privacy? Consent? |
| **E** | Environmental | Land, forests, rivers, climate, biodiversity, jhum cultivation |

Write P-E-S-T-L-E vertically on your rough sheet, spend 4 minutes writing 1–2 bullets
next to each, then **pick the best three or four** as your body paragraphs. You now have
an essay. You have not needed a single memorised fact.

#### Framework 2 — Past / Present / Future (gives you *movement*)

Every good essay moves in time.
- **Past**: how did this come about? (¶2 of Skeleton B)
- **Present**: what is the situation now, with its tensions? (body)
- **Future**: what should be done, and by whom? (way forward)

An essay with all three feels complete. An essay stuck in the present feels thin.

#### Framework 3 — the stakeholder sweep (use when PESTLE runs dry)

List who is affected: **citizen / farmer / student / woman / youth / worker / business /
state government / central government / civil society / future generations.**
Each stakeholder is a potential paragraph or at minimum a sentence.

#### The 8-minute planning ritual (do exactly this)

```
min 0–1  Read all topic options. Pick. Underline the KEY NOUN and the KEY VERB
         in the topic. ("ROLE of technology in RURAL development" — you must
         discuss role, and you must discuss rural.)
min 1–5  PESTLE dump on rough sheet. Past/Present/Future dump.
min 5–7  Choose 5–7 paragraphs. Number them. Write ONE phrase per paragraph
         = your topic sentence.
min 7–8  Write your thesis sentence in full, and your closing line in full.
         (Knowing your last line before you start is a huge stabiliser.)
```

Then write. Do not deviate from the plan even if a better idea arrives at minute 30.

### 2.7 Using facts, data and quotes

- **A few precise facts beat many vague ones.** Two or three concrete anchors in a
  900-word essay is enough. Ten unsupported statistics look invented.
- **Only use numbers you are sure of.** A wrong statistic is worse than no statistic —
  it signals you are bluffing. If you are unsure, use a *qualitative* claim instead:
  "a large share of", "the majority of", "roughly half". These cannot be wrong.
- **Safe anchors you can reliably use** (verify the ones you plan to lean on):
  - Constitutional articles: Art. 14 equality, Art. 19 freedoms, Art. 21 life and
    personal liberty, Art. 21A education, Art. 51A fundamental duties, **Art. 371A
    special provisions for Nagaland**, Art. 243 Panchayati Raj.
  - Named schemes and missions (no numbers needed): Digital India, MGNREGA, Ayushman
    Bharat, PM Jan Dhan Yojana, Swachh Bharat, National Education Policy 2020,
    Act East Policy, Skill India, Startup India, Jal Jeevan Mission.
  - Institutions: NITI Aayog, RBI, Election Commission, CAG, Supreme Court,
    NEC (North Eastern Council), DoNER Ministry.
  - Nagaland-specific: Article 371A; the Nagaland Village Council & Village
    Development Board system; the Communitisation of Public Institutions and Services
    Act, 2002 (a genuinely distinctive Nagaland innovation and an excellent essay
    example); Hornbill Festival; Act East Policy and the state's border with Myanmar.
- **Quotations**: one is enough, two maximum. See `02_ESSAY_BANK.md` for a bank of 20.
  Attribute correctly or attribute vaguely ("as an old proverb has it") — never
  misattribute to a famous name.

### 2.8 Common mistakes that lose marks

| Mistake | Cost | Fix |
|---|:---:|---|
| No paragraph breaks / one giant block | −3 | Break every 120–150 words |
| Ignoring half the topic ("causes **and** remedies") | −4 | Underline every clause in the question |
| One-sided rant | −2 | Always one "However…" paragraph |
| Repeating the same idea in ¶3 and ¶6 | −2 | Follow the plan; each ¶ has one job |
| Very long sentences (40+ words) | −2 | Aim for average 15–20 words; vary length |
| Overwriting, scratching out, no margin | −2 | Cross out with a single clean line |
| Running to 1500 words and losing time elsewhere | −6 elsewhere | Line-count discipline |
| Sweeping unverifiable statistics | −2 | Qualitative language instead |
| Slang, SMS abbreviations, "&", "etc." repeatedly | −1 | Formal register throughout |
| Starting with "Since time immemorial…" | −1 | Use a real hook |

### 2.9 Handwriting and presentation

Presentation is worth roughly 2 marks and costs nothing.

- **Legibility beats beauty.** If the examiner has to slow down, you lose.
- Leave a **left margin** and a blank line between paragraphs.
- **Underline your essay title.** Underline nothing else except, at most, 2–3 key terms.
  An essay with fifteen underlines looks frantic.
- Write the **word count at the end** if you are near target — it signals control.
  Skip it if you overshot.
- Use **black or blue ink only**; one colour throughout.
- If you must delete, one horizontal line. Never scribble a blob.
- **Numbering**: label your answers clearly with the question number. An unlabelled
  answer can be missed entirely.

### 2.10 Practice plan for the essay in 21 days

| | |
|---|---|
| Days 1–3 | Read the 4 model essays in `02_ESSAY_BANK.md`. Copy out one introduction and one conclusion by hand to internalise the rhythm. |
| Days 4–14 | **One full timed essay every alternate day (5 total).** 50 minutes, no phone, handwritten. Pick from the topic bank, alternating themes. |
| Days 4–14 (off days) | 10-minute drill: pick a topic, do *only* the 8-minute plan. No writing. Do 8–10 of these. Plans are cheap and build the reflex. |
| Days 15–19 | 2 more timed essays; one must be a Nagaland/North-East topic. |
| Days 20–21 | No new essays. Re-read your own plans and the quotation bank. |

## 3. PRÉCIS WRITING — 10 marks

### 3.1 What a précis is, in plain English

You are given a passage of maybe 350–450 words. You must rewrite it in about
**one-third** of its length, keeping *every essential idea* and *nothing else*, in
**your own words**, in **your own continuous prose**, and give it a **title**.

It is not a summary you write casually. It has strict conventions, and most of the
10 marks are for **following the conventions**, not for elegance. This is the single
most *mechanically learnable* item on the English half of the paper. A candidate who
memorises the rules should score 7–8/10 reliably.

### 3.2 The strict rules

| # | Rule | Note |
|:---:|---|---|
| 1 | **Length ≈ one-third of the original.** | If the passage is 360 words, write 115–125. Tolerance is roughly ±10%. Being 50% too long is the commonest failure. |
| 2 | **Always give a title.** | Short — 3 to 7 words. Written above, centred or left, underlined. A missing title costs a mark outright. |
| 3 | **Write in the third person.** | "I believe…" in the passage becomes "The writer believes…" or simply "It is argued that…". Never carry "I", "we", "you" across. |
| 4 | **Write in the past tense** — for narrative and reported opinion. | Universal truths and permanent facts stay in the present ("Water boils at 100°C"; "The Himalayas lie to the north"). |
| 5 | **Use your own words.** | Some technical terms must be reused (you cannot paraphrase "photosynthesis"). But do not lift whole sentences. |
| 6 | **No new material.** | No examples of your own, no opinions of your own, no facts from outside the passage. |
| 7 | **No direct speech, no quotations, no dialogue.** | Convert to indirect speech. |
| 8 | **One continuous paragraph.** | A précis is never bulleted and rarely more than one paragraph. |
| 9 | **Keep the author's proportion and order.** | If a third of the passage is about causes, roughly a third of your précis is about causes. |
| 10 | **No rhetorical questions, no exclamations.** | Convert "Is this acceptable?" to "The writer questions whether this is acceptable." |
| 11 | **Cut all: examples, illustrations, repetitions, figures of speech, digressions, adjectives of emphasis.** | This is where your compression comes from. |
| 12 | **State the word count at the end** in brackets. | e.g. `(118 words)`. Shows you obeyed rule 1. |

### 3.3 Step-by-step method (the 25-minute procedure)

**Step 1 — Read once, fast (2 min).** Do not write. Just get the gist. Ask: *what is
this passage arguing?*

**Step 2 — Read again with a pencil (4 min).**
- Underline the **topic sentence** of each paragraph.
- **Strike through** every example, every "for instance", every list of illustrations,
  every quotation, every repetition.
- Circle **connective words** (however, therefore, moreover) — they carry the logical
  skeleton and you must preserve the logic, if not the words.

**Step 3 — Count the original (1 min).**
Count the words in one full line, multiply by the number of lines. Divide by three.
Write your target on the rough sheet: e.g. `Target: 120 words (±12)`.

**Step 4 — Write the skeleton in the margin (3 min).**
Reduce each paragraph to one clause. Four paragraphs → four clauses. That is your
précis in miniature.

**Step 5 — Draft in rough (6 min).** Join the clauses into flowing prose. Third person,
past tense. Do not look back at the passage while drafting — this is what forces "own
words".

**Step 6 — Count and trim (3 min).** Over the target? Cut adjectives first, then
merge sentences with participles ("Having considered X, the writer argues Y").

**Step 7 — Write the fair copy + title (5 min).** Title last, once you know what the
passage is really about. Add `(N words)`.

**Step 8 — Run the checklist (1 min).** See §3.6.

### 3.4 Compression techniques (how you actually get to one-third)

| Technique | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Phrase → word | "in spite of the fact that" | "although" |
| List → category | "rice, maize, millet and wheat" | "cereals" |
| Clause → participle | "He finished the report and then submitted it." | "Having finished the report, he submitted it." |
| Drop the illustration | "Pollution is severe — in Delhi, for example, …" | "Pollution is severe." |
| Two sentences → one | "The scheme failed. It lacked funds." | "The scheme failed for want of funds." |
| Drop intensifiers | "extremely important and highly significant" | "important" |
| Nominalise | "when the government decided to intervene" | "on government intervention" |

### 3.5 Worked example

#### The passage (352 words)

> Every year, in the hill districts of the North-East, the rains arrive with a
> ferocity that outsiders find difficult to imagine. Roads that took three years to
> build vanish in a single night. Culverts collapse. Villages that were a two-hour
> drive from the district headquarters become, for weeks at a time, effectively
> islands. I have watched a schoolteacher in one such village walk eleven kilometres
> along a ridge because the road below had simply ceased to exist.
>
> The usual response is to blame the contractor. Sometimes that blame is deserved.
> But the deeper problem is that we have been building the wrong kind of road in the
> wrong kind of place. Engineering practice in India was largely codified for the
> plains, where the chief enemies are traffic load and temperature. In young, folded,
> heavily-rained-upon mountains, the chief enemy is water — water moving through soil,
> water undercutting slopes, water finding every seam in the rock. A road that is
> merely strong is not enough. A road must also drain.
>
> There is, moreover, a question of who builds. For decades, hill roads have been
> designed in distant offices by engineers who have never walked the alignment in
> August. Local knowledge — which slope slips, which spring runs all year, where the
> old footpath ran and why it ran there — is treated as folklore rather than as data.
> Yet villagers can often predict a landslide site with an accuracy that would
> embarrass a soil report.
>
> The remedy is not more money alone, though more money is needed. It is a change of
> method: design that begins with drainage rather than ending with it; specifications
> written for mountains rather than borrowed from the plains; and a genuine role for
> the village in choosing and maintaining its own alignment. Where communities have
> been given charge of small assets — as some states in the region have experimented
> with — maintenance has often improved dramatically, because the asset belongs to
> somebody. Until that shift happens, we will keep rebuilding the same road, and the
> rain will keep taking it away.

#### Model précis (117 words — one-third of 352 ≈ 117)

> **Title: Why Hill Roads Fail**
>
> The writer observed that heavy monsoon rain in North-Eastern hill districts
> repeatedly destroyed newly built roads and cut villages off for weeks. While
> contractors were often blamed, he argued that the deeper fault lay in method:
> Indian engineering practice was framed for the plains, where load and temperature
> matter most, whereas in young, high-rainfall mountains the decisive threat is
> water. A road therefore had to drain as well as bear weight. He further noted that
> alignments were designed remotely by engineers unfamiliar with the terrain, and
> that villagers' knowledge of unstable slopes was dismissed. The remedy he proposed
> was drainage-led design, mountain-specific specifications, and community
> responsibility for choosing and maintaining roads.
>
> *(117 words)*

#### What that précis did, move by move

- **Title** given, short, captures the argument (not just the subject).
- "I have watched a schoolteacher…" — a pure illustration — **deleted entirely**.
- First person "I", "we" → **"the writer"**, third person throughout.
- Past tense for the writer's reported argument ("argued", "noted", "proposed");
  but the general truth "the decisive threat is water" kept in the present. Both are
  correct.
- Order of the original (problem → misdiagnosis → real cause → who builds → remedy)
  **preserved exactly**.
- No new material: the "some states in the region" clause was generalised, not
  turned into a named example.
- Rhetorical richness ("the rain will keep taking it away") dropped — précis is plain.

### 3.6 The pre-submission checklist

Tick all nine before you move on:

- [ ] Title written and underlined
- [ ] Length within ±10% of one-third
- [ ] Word count stated in brackets
- [ ] Third person — no "I", "we", "you", "our"
- [ ] Past tense for reported argument; present only for permanent truths
- [ ] One continuous paragraph, no bullets, no headings
- [ ] No quotations, no direct speech, no rhetorical questions
- [ ] No examples, no illustrations, no repetition
- [ ] Nothing in it that was not in the passage

### 3.7 Practice plan

Find any 300–450 word passage — a newspaper editorial is ideal — and précis it in
25 minutes. **Do six of these across the 21 days.** Editorials from *The Hindu*,
*Indian Express* or *Nagaland Post* work perfectly and double as current-affairs
revision. Precision comes from repetition, not from reading about précis.

## 4. AMPLIFICATION — 10 marks

### 4.1 What amplification is (most candidates get this wrong)

You are given a **proverb, maxim, quotation or short saying** — for example
*"A stitch in time saves nine"* or *"Knowledge is power"* — and asked to **amplify** it.

**Amplification means: expand this compressed statement into a developed piece of
prose that explains what it means, why it is true, and where it applies.**

It is **not**:
- ❌ a mini-essay on a topic (you must stay tethered to the saying);
- ❌ a definition (too short);
- ❌ a story (an example is one *part* of it, not the whole);
- ❌ a précis (that is compression; this is expansion — the opposite).

Think of it as: *the saying is a seed; you grow it into a plant of five parts.*

### 4.2 Length and format

| | |
|---|---|
| **Words** | **200–300** (250 is the sweet spot) |
| **Paragraphs** | 3–5 short paragraphs, or one well-signposted block |
| **Time** | 20 minutes total |
| **Opening** | Quote the maxim itself in the first line, in quotation marks |
| **Heading** | Write the maxim as the heading, underlined |

Do not write 600 words. Amplification is worth 10 marks — the same as grammar. Match
your effort to the marks.

### 4.3 The five-move structure (memorise this)

| Move | What you write | ~Words |
|:---:|---|---:|
| **1. Literal meaning** | What the words say on their face. Unpack the image or metaphor concretely. | 40 |
| **2. Deeper / general meaning** | The universal principle behind the image. This is the heart of the answer. | 60 |
| **3. Illustration** | One or two concrete examples — historical, everyday, scientific, or from public life. **This is where most candidates are weakest and where marks are easiest.** | 70 |
| **4. Contemporary relevance** | Why this matters now — link to modern life, technology, governance, society, India today. | 50 |
| **5. Conclusion** | Restate the maxim in your own words, elevated. One or two sentences. | 30 |

**The single most useful tip:** move 3 (illustration) is what separates a 5/10 from an
8/10. Have a stock of examples ready that can be bent to almost any maxim — see §4.5.

### 4.4 Worked example 1 — *"A stitch in time saves nine"*

> ### "A stitch in time saves nine"
>
> Taken literally, the proverb describes a piece of cloth in which a single thread has
> given way. If the small tear is mended at once, one stitch is enough. If it is
> ignored, the tear widens with every use until nine stitches, or a new garment
> altogether, are required. The image is domestic, but the logic is universal.
>
> The deeper meaning is that problems have a cost curve that rises with delay.
> Difficulty is rarely static; neglect compounds it. A small defect attended to early
> consumes a little effort at a moment when effort is cheap. The same defect left
> alone draws in other systems, hardens into habit, and eventually demands resources
> far beyond what timely action would have required. Promptness, the proverb suggests,
> is not merely a virtue of character but a form of economy.
>
> Illustrations are everywhere. In medicine, a condition detected at screening is
> often curable, while the same condition detected late may be untreatable at any
> price — which is precisely the argument for preventive health programmes. In
> engineering, a hairline crack in a bridge or a small unpatched flaw in software
> costs little to repair and a great deal to ignore; most large system failures begin
> as a small fault that nobody thought worth attending to. In public administration, a
> minor local grievance addressed within a week may never be heard of again, while the
> same grievance ignored for a year can become a movement.
>
> The saying has gained rather than lost force in our own time. Modern systems are
> tightly coupled, so a small failure propagates faster than it once did. Climate
> change is perhaps the largest example of a stitch not taken in time: emissions that
> could have been curbed cheaply decades ago now demand a far costlier global effort.
> The same holds in personal life, where a postponed examination syllabus or an
> unattended savings habit exacts its price later.
>
> In short, the proverb counsels vigilance rather than mere haste. To act early is
> not to act anxiously; it is to recognise that time itself is an ingredient in the
> cost of every problem. The stitch taken today is the cheapest stitch we will ever
> take.
>
> *(≈340 words — trim the third paragraph to two examples for a 250-word version.)*

### 4.5 Worked example 2 — *"Knowledge is power"*

> ### "Knowledge is power"
>
> The phrase, associated with Francis Bacon, sets two words side by side that at first
> seem unrelated. Literally it claims that knowing something — how a thing works, what
> the law says, where the money went — confers the capacity to act upon it. Power here
> is not force but the ability to bring about intended effects.
>
> At a deeper level the maxim describes the relationship between information and
> control. The person who understands a system can predict it; the person who can
> predict it can shape it. This is why, historically, knowledge has been guarded:
> priesthoods restricted scripture, guilds restricted craft secrets, and colonial
> administrations restricted technical education. Conversely, every genuine expansion
> of freedom in history has been accompanied by an expansion of literacy. Ignorance is
> not a neutral absence; it is a condition in which one is acted upon rather than
> acting.
>
> The illustrations are striking. The scientific revolution converted knowledge of
> nature into power over nature — medicine, electricity, agriculture. In India, the
> Right to Information Act, 2005 rests on exactly this proposition: it gives the
> ordinary citizen access to official records, and in doing so shifts a measure of
> power from the office to the applicant. At an individual level, a farmer who knows
> the market price of his crop bargains differently from one who does not.
>
> In the present century the maxim needs a qualification. We live amid an
> unprecedented abundance of information, yet abundance is not knowledge. Data must be
> verified, understood and applied before it becomes power; unverified information can
> instead be used to manipulate, as the spread of misinformation online demonstrates.
> Moreover, knowledge concentrated in a few hands — in large technology firms or in
> the state — produces power that is equally concentrated. The democratic task of our
> time is therefore not only to create knowledge but to distribute it.
>
> Knowledge, then, is power only when it is true, understood, and shared. Held by the
> few it is dominance; held by the many it is freedom.
>
> *(≈330 words)*

### 4.6 Worked example 3 — *"United we stand, divided we fall"*

> ### "United we stand, divided we fall"
>
> The literal picture is military. A body of people standing together presents an
> unbroken front and cannot easily be pushed over; the same people scattered are
> overcome one by one. The saying compresses a fact about physical formations into a
> rule about human affairs.
>
> Its deeper claim is that collective strength is not simply the sum of individual
> strengths. Unity adds something — coordination, mutual support, shared purpose —
> that the same individuals acting separately do not possess. Division subtracts
> correspondingly, because a divided group spends against itself the energy it would
> otherwise spend on its common problem. This is why those who wish to weaken a
> community rarely attack it directly; they set its parts against one another.
>
> History supplies both halves of the lesson. India's freedom struggle became
> irresistible only when it drew together regions, languages, faiths and classes into
> a single movement; the same subcontinent had earlier been subdued piecemeal because
> its kingdoms would not combine. The formation of cooperative societies, of trade
> unions, and of self-help groups follows the same principle at a smaller scale. In
> the North-East, the practical value of unity is visible in the village institutions
> that manage common forests, water sources and footpaths — assets no single household
> could maintain but which a community sustains without difficulty.
>
> Today the maxim applies to problems that respect no boundary. Pandemics, climate
> change and cybercrime cannot be answered by any one state acting alone, which is why
> international cooperation, however imperfect, persists. Within a country, unity
> means something more demanding than uniformity: it means holding together across
> real differences of tribe, language and belief, and resisting the easy politics that
> profits from sharpening those differences.
>
> Unity, properly understood, is therefore not the absence of difference but the
> presence of a shared purpose strong enough to carry difference. That is what allows
> a people to stand; its loss is what makes them fall.
>
> *(≈325 words)*

## 5. CONSTRUCTION OF SENTENCE & GRAMMAR — 10 marks

Cheapest marks on the paper. Target **9/10**. This section is pure recall and pattern
recognition, which is exactly what an engineer's brain is good at. Two hours of study
here is worth more than ten hours on the essay.

### 5.1 Articles (a / an / the)

**The rule in plain English:** `a/an` = one of many, first mention, unspecified.
`the` = we both know which one. No article = general/uncountable/plural concepts.

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| `an` before a **vowel sound**, not a vowel letter | an hour, an MP, an honest man **but** a university, a European, a one-rupee coin |
| `the` before superlatives, ordinals, unique things | the best, the first, the sun, the Constitution |
| `the` before rivers, seas, mountain **ranges**, groups of islands | the Ganga, the Himalayas, the Andamans **but** Mount Everest (single peak) |
| `the` before nationalities/adjectives used as nouns | the rich, the poor, the Naga people |
| **No article** before abstract nouns, meals, languages, most countries | Honesty pays. He had lunch. She speaks English. He went to India. |
| `the` for a class taken as a whole | The tiger is a wild animal. |
| `the` before names of newspapers, ships, holy books | the Hindu, the Titanic, the Bible |
| No article with "go to school/college/hospital/bed/prison" *for their purpose* | He went to school. **but** His father went to the school to meet the teacher. |

### 5.3 Subject–verb agreement

**The rule:** the verb agrees with the **subject**, not with whatever noun happens to
sit nearest to it.

| Pattern | Correct form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Subject + *with / along with / as well as / together with / including / besides* + noun | verb agrees with the **first** subject | The minister, **along with** his officers, **was** present. |
| Either…or / Neither…nor | verb agrees with the **nearer** subject | Neither the students nor the teacher **was** informed. |
| Two subjects joined by **and** | plural | Ram and Shyam **are** here. |
| …but one **idea** joined by and | singular | Bread and butter **is** my breakfast. |
| Each / Every / Either / Neither / Everyone / Nobody / Anyone | **singular** | Each of the boys **has** a book. |
| **A number of** X | plural | A number of students **were** absent. |
| **The number of** X | singular | The number of students **was** fifty. |
| Collective nouns (team, committee, jury) acting as one | singular | The committee **has** decided. |
| …acting as individuals | plural | The committee **are** divided in their views. |
| Uncountables: news, mathematics, physics, politics, economics, measles | **singular** | The news **is** good. Politics **is** dirty. |
| Always plural: scissors, trousers, spectacles, cattle, police, people | plural | The police **have** arrived. Cattle **are** grazing. |
| Distance / money / time as one amount | singular | Ten kilometres **is** a long walk. Five lakh rupees **is** a large sum. |
| One of + plural noun + who/that | verb after "who" is **plural** | He is one of those men who **are** never satisfied. |
| Titles of books, names of countries | singular | *Gulliver's Travels* **is** a classic. The United States **is** … |

### 5.4 Tenses — the 12 forms and what each is for

| | Simple | Continuous | Perfect | Perfect Continuous |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Past** | I wrote | I was writing | I had written | I had been writing |
| **Present** | I write | I am writing | I have written | I have been writing |
| **Future** | I shall/will write | I shall be writing | I shall have written | I shall have been writing |

**High-yield tense rules:**

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| **Since / for + present perfect continuous** for something still going on | He **has been living** here **since** 2019. (not "is living since") |
| Completed action with a **past time marker** → simple past | I **saw** him yesterday. (not "have seen him yesterday") |
| Two past actions → the earlier one takes **past perfect** | The train **had left** before I **reached** the station. |
| After "**If**" (2nd conditional) → past subjunctive + would | If I **were** you, I **would** accept. |
| Conditional type 1 | If it **rains**, we **shall** stay in. |
| Conditional type 3 | If he **had studied**, he **would have passed**. |
| **No future tense after** when/if/until/before/as soon as | I will call you when he **comes**. (not "will come") |
| Universal truths → **present simple always** | The teacher said that the earth **moves** round the sun. |
| **"It is time / I wish"** → past tense form | It is time we **went** home. I wish I **knew** the answer. |
| Verbs of state (know, believe, own, seem, contain, love) → **not** continuous | I **know** him. (not "I am knowing him") |

### 5.5 Active and passive voice

**Conversion rule:** the **object** of the active sentence becomes the **subject** of the
passive; the verb becomes `be + past participle`; the old subject goes into a `by` phrase
(often dropped).

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Present simple | He writes a letter. | A letter **is written** by him. |
| Present continuous | He is writing a letter. | A letter **is being written** by him. |
| Present perfect | He has written a letter. | A letter **has been written** by him. |
| Past simple | He wrote a letter. | A letter **was written** by him. |
| Past continuous | He was writing a letter. | A letter **was being written** by him. |
| Past perfect | He had written a letter. | A letter **had been written** by him. |
| Future simple | He will write a letter. | A letter **will be written** by him. |
| Future perfect | He will have written a letter. | A letter **will have been written** by him. |
| Modal | He can write a letter. | A letter **can be written** by him. |
| Imperative (order) | Shut the door. | **Let** the door **be shut**. |
| Imperative (request) | Please help me. | You are **requested to help** me. |
| Question (do) | Did he write it? | **Was** it **written** by him? |
| Question (wh-) | Who wrote it? | **By whom was** it written? |
| Infinitive | I have a job to do. | I have a job **to be done**. |

> **Note:** perfect continuous and future continuous have no natural passive. If asked,
> the question is faulty — don't panic, rephrase.

### 5.6 Direct and indirect speech

**Three things change:** the **tense** (back-shift), the **pronouns**, and the
**words of time and place**.

**Reporting verb in the past → back-shift:**

| Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|
| present simple | past simple |
| present continuous | past continuous |
| present perfect | past perfect |
| past simple | past perfect |
| will | would |
| can | could |
| may | might |
| must | had to / must |
| shall | should / would |

**No back-shift if:** the reporting verb is in the present/future, or the statement is
a universal truth, or a historical fact.

**Time & place words:**

| Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|
| now | then |
| today | that day |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day |
| yesterday | the previous day / the day before |
| last night | the previous night |
| next week | the following week |
| here | there |
| this | that |
| these | those |
| ago | before |
| thus | so |

**Sentence-type conversions:**

| Type | Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|---|
| Statement | He said, "I am tired." | He said **that** he **was** tired. |
| Yes/no question | He said, "Are you ready?" | He **asked if/whether** I **was** ready. |
| Wh- question | He said, "Where do you live?" | He **asked where** I **lived**. |
| Command | He said, "Shut the door." | He **ordered** me **to shut** the door. |
| Request | He said, "Please help me." | He **requested** me **to help** him. |
| Advice | He said, "You should rest." | He **advised** me **to rest**. |
| Negative command | He said, "Don't go." | He **forbade** me **to go** / told me **not to go**. |
| Exclamation | He said, "What a fine day!" | He **exclaimed that** it **was** a very fine day. |
| Wish | He said, "May you succeed!" | He **wished that** I **might** succeed. |

> In questions, the reported clause takes **statement word order** — "he asked where I
> lived", never "he asked where did I live". This is the most common error.

### 5.10 Error spotting — the systematic method

Most error-spotting questions test one of only **eight** things. Check them in this
order and you will find the error faster than by "reading for what sounds wrong":

1. **Subject–verb agreement** (find the true subject; ignore intervening phrases)
2. **Tense consistency** (any past marker? any two-past-actions structure?)
3. **Preposition** (is it on the trap list in §5.2?)
4. **Article** (missing `the` before a superlative? `a` before a vowel sound?)
5. **Pronoun** (does it agree in number? "Everyone…their" is loose; exams prefer "his")
6. **Parallelism** ("He likes reading, writing and **to swim**" → "and swimming")
7. **Redundancy** (return back, repeat again, more better, cope up with, discuss about, reason is because)
8. **Word choice / confusable** (§5.7)

### 5.11 Sentence transformation — the standard conversions

| From | To | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple → Complex | insert a clause | *In spite of his poverty, he is happy.* → *Though he is poor, he is happy.* |
| Complex → Compound | use and/but/so | *Though he is poor, he is happy.* → *He is poor **but** he is happy.* |
| Affirmative → Negative | use "no…other / not…any" | *He is the best boy.* → *No other boy is as good as he.* |
| Assertive → Interrogative | rhetorical question | *Everybody wishes to be happy.* → *Who does not wish to be happy?* |
| Exclamatory → Assertive | — | *How beautiful the sky is!* → *The sky is very beautiful.* |
| Degrees: Positive → Comparative → Superlative | — | *No other city is as large as Mumbai.* → *Mumbai is larger than any other city.* → *Mumbai is the largest city.* |
| Too…to → so…that…not | — | *He is too weak to walk.* → *He is so weak that he cannot walk.* |

3. **the** (unique — "the capital of")
4. **a** ("university" begins with a /juː/ consonant sound)
5. **—** (no article before an abstract noun used generally)
6. **on** — congratulate *on*
7. **to** — senior *to* (Latin comparatives take "to", never "than")
8. **—** (no preposition; "discuss the matter". "Discuss about" is wrong.)
9. **of** — die *of* a disease
10. **to** — prefer X *to* Y
11. **was** — "along with" does not make the subject plural
12. **has** — with neither…nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject ("teacher")
13. **is** — "the number of" is singular
14. **is** — Physics is singular despite the -s
15. **is** — a single quantity of distance
16. He **has been living** in Dimapur since 2018.
17. I **saw** him yesterday.
18. The train had left before I **reached**.
19. If I **were** you, I would resign.
20. I will call you when he **comes**.
21. The proposal **has been approved** by the committee.
22. **Let the window be shut.**
23. **By whom was the glass broken?**
24. He **asked me where I was going.**
25. She said **that she would finish the work the next day.**
26. **have → has** ("Each of the boys **has**"). Also "their" is loose; "his" is preferred.
27. **is → are** ("one of those men **who are** never satisfied" — "who" refers to "men")
28. **is because → is that** ("The reason … is that he did not study." "Reason … because" is redundant.)
29. **bureaucracy**
30. **polyglot**

</details>

### 5.13 How to study this section in 21 days

- **Days 1–4:** read §5.1–5.7 once each day. Do not memorise — just re-read. Familiarity
  is enough for 80% of these.
- **Days 5–14:** 20 minutes a day on §5.8–5.9 (idioms and one-word substitutions).
  These are pure vocabulary and need spaced repetition. Make 5 flashcards a day.
- **Days 15–20:** do the practice set again from cold, plus any grammar questions from
  the PYQ folder. Target: under 15 minutes, 27+/30.
- **Day 21:** skim the tables only. No new material.

## 6. GENERAL KNOWLEDGE — 50 marks

Half the paper. This is where 21 days of study actually move the needle.

### 6.1 The seven areas and how to prioritise them

The official wording lists: **current events, general science, mental ability, Indian
history, Indian polity, Indian economy, Geography.** Assume roughly equal weight
(~7 marks each) unless the PYQs in your `PYQ/` folder say otherwise — check them.

Priority for **a technical candidate with 21 days**:

| Rank | Area | Why this rank | Days to give it |
|:---:|---|---|:---:|
| **1** | **Mental Ability** | You are an engineer. This is arithmetic and logic. You can go from 0 to near-full marks in ~2 days because the question types are finite and mechanical. **Highest marks per hour on the entire paper.** | 2 |
| **2** | **Indian Polity** | Small, closed, highly predictable syllabus. Constitution facts repeat year after year. And **Article 371A is near-certain in a Nagaland state exam**. | 3 |
| **3** | **Nagaland / North-East GK** | Not named separately in the syllabus, but a state PSC *always* embeds state questions inside history/geography/polity/current events. Almost nobody prepares this systematically. Highest-yield, least-competed. | 3 |
| **4** | **General Science** | You already know most of the physics and chemistry. Only biology needs real work (human body, diseases, vitamins, nutrition). | 2 |
| **5** | **Geography** | Learnable in tables — rivers, ranges, soils, crops, minerals. Plus Nagaland geography, which overlaps with #3. | 2 |
| **6** | **Indian Economy** | Concept-heavy but the exam-level questions are shallow: inflation, GDP, RBI, budget, schemes. | 2 |
| **7** | **Indian History** | The largest syllabus with the lowest return per hour. **Restrict yourself to modern India (1857–1947) plus a thin skim of ancient/medieval.** Do not attempt to master it. | 3 |
| **8** | **Current Affairs** | Cannot be crammed, but a targeted sweep of the last 12 months pays. Do it in short daily doses, not one block. | 30 min daily |

### 6.3 How to answer "short descriptive" GK questions

This is the format most technical candidates handle badly. A short-descriptive GK
question is typically 3 or 5 marks and asks something like:

> *"Write a short note on Article 371A of the Indian Constitution."* (5)
> *"What is inflation? Briefly explain its causes."* (3)
> *"Mention any four features of the Preamble."* (4)

**The universal template:**

```
[Definition / what it is — 1 sentence]
[Key facts, as 3–5 short numbered or bulleted points]
[Significance / why it matters — 1 sentence]
```

**Rules of thumb:**

| Marks | Words | Structure |
|:---:|:---:|---|
| 2 | 30–40 | One definition sentence + two facts |
| 3 | 50–70 | Definition + 3 bullets |
| 5 | 90–130 | One-line definition + heading-led bullets + a closing significance line |
| 8–10 | 200–250 | Short intro ¶ + 3 sub-headings + conclusion |

**Do:**
- **Use the exact keyword the examiner is scanning for.** For Art. 371A, the words
  "religious or social practices of the Nagas", "customary law and procedure",
  "administration of civil and criminal justice", "ownership and transfer of land and
  its resources", "resolution of the Legislative Assembly" are the marks. Get them in.
- **Use bullets and sub-headings** for anything 4 marks and above. Examiners marking
  200 scripts find bullets faster than prose, and speed of finding = marks.
- **Underline the key term once** in your answer.
- **Answer the exact number asked.** "Any four features" → write exactly four, clearly
  numbered. Writing seven wastes time and can invite a penalty if some are wrong.

**Don't:**
- Don't write an essay. A 3-mark question answered in 300 words costs you a 5-mark
  question elsewhere.
- Don't waffle before the definition. Start with the answer in the first line.
- Don't leave anything blank. A partially remembered fact scores more than a blank.

**Worked example — a 5-mark answer:**

> **Q. Write a short note on Article 371A.** (5)
>
> **Ans.** Article 371A of the Constitution contains special provisions for the
> State of Nagaland. It was inserted by the **Constitution (Thirteenth Amendment)
> Act, 1962**, giving effect to the 16-Point Agreement between the Government of
> India and the Naga People's Convention, and came into force with Nagaland's
> statehood on 1 December 1963.
>
> Its main features are:
> 1. **No Act of Parliament** shall apply to Nagaland in respect of —
>    (a) religious or social practices of the Nagas, (b) Naga customary law and
>    procedure, (c) administration of civil and criminal justice involving decisions
>    according to Naga customary law, and (d) ownership and transfer of land and its
>    resources — **unless the Legislative Assembly of Nagaland so decides by a
>    resolution**.
> 2. The **Governor of Nagaland** has special responsibility for law and order in the
>    State so long as internal disturbances continue, exercising his individual
>    judgement after consulting the Council of Ministers.
> 3. It provides for a **Regional Council for the Tuensang district** and special
>    transitional arrangements for that area.
>
> **Significance:** Article 371A constitutionally protects Naga customary law, land
> rights and social practices, and is regarded as the cornerstone of Nagaland's
> distinct political and legal identity within the Indian Union.

Notice: definition first, the amendment number and year, the four protected subjects
verbatim, the Governor's role, and a one-line significance. That is a full-marks answer
and it takes four minutes to write.

### 6.4 Objective GK — tactics

- **No stated negative marking on this paper.** (Negative marking is specified only for
  the Technical MCQ paper.) So **attempt every single question.**
- Eliminate first. Two options are usually obviously wrong; a 50/50 guess is worth
  taking.
- Beware "All of the above" — it is correct more often than chance, but only when you
  can verify at least two options.
- Extremes ("always", "never", "only") in an option usually signal a wrong option.
- Numbers: if three options cluster and one is an outlier, the answer is usually in
  the cluster.
- **Do not go back and change an answer** unless you have a positive reason. First
  instinct on factual recall is right more often than second-guessing.

## 7. The final 72 hours

**Day 19 (24 Sept):** Full 3-hour mock. Same start time as the exam (9:00 am) if you
can. Write it by hand, in one sitting, with a real clock. This is the single most
valuable study session in the entire 21 days — it calibrates your speed, which is the
only thing you cannot learn from a book.

**Day 20 (25 Sept):**
- Morning: review the mock. Note exactly where you overran time.
- Afternoon: revise the GK capsule tables only — Polity, Nagaland, Geography.
- Evening: re-read the essay skeletons, the précis rules, the amplification 5-move
  structure. Read the quotation bank once.
- Pack: admit card, photo ID, pens (3 of the same colour), spare refills, watch,
  transparent water bottle. Check the venue location and travel time.
- **Sleep by 22:00.** No new material after 20:00.

**Day 21 (26 Sept — exam day):**
- Light breakfast. Nothing heavy or unfamiliar.
- Arrive 45 minutes early.
- First 60 seconds in the hall: **read the essay topics and pick one.** Then start with
  grammar.
- Write your three clock checkpoints (§1.4) on the rough sheet before you write anything
  else.
- If you blank on a GK question, move on immediately. There are fifty marks of GK and
  none of them are worth two minutes of staring.
